Current Situation

The Shortage Pressure Index (SPI) sits at 65.1, remaining in the elevated band. Today's pressure is heavily concentrated in petroleum and liquid fuels, where the hard data index ($D = 1.51$) reflects sustained stress in both upstream crude prices and downstream refined products. Brent crude is trading at $107.03 per barrel (+62.7% YoY) and WTI at $99.64 per barrel (+61.3% YoY). Domestically, regular retail gasoline averages $4.153 per gallon (+37.6% YoY), while retail diesel remains heavily stressed at $6.292 per gallon (+67.7% YoY). The US Strategic Petroleum Reserve stands at 282.18 million barrels, down 31.1% from a year ago.

By contrast, other essential household categories remain tranquil. Natural gas ($D = -0.02$) and fertilizer ($D = 0.00$) show neutral readings. Food metrics ($D = 0.72$) reflect elevated international commodity values—world wheat and rice are both roughly 37% higher year-over-year, and acute international food insecurity reaches 159.85 million people—yet domestic supply lines remain stable. Contiguous US drought covers 43.98% of land area, which is 4.6 percentage points below the five-year seasonal average.

Why These Probabilities?

Qualifying US shortages are historically rare events that require severe physical dislocations, such as major pipeline disruptions, multi-state refinery shutdowns, or broad supermarket purchase quotas. While fuel prices are elevated, physical distribution to retail pumps remains functional, which keeps the 1-week fuel shortage probability low at 4.0%. However, because maritime traffic through the Strait of Hormuz remains constrained at roughly 4.4 daily transits (compared to normal levels above 60), the medium-term probability of a qualifying fuel disruption reaches 28.0% at 3 months and 45.0% at 1 year.

Food shortage risks remain modest (1.5% at 1 month, 4.0% at 3 months) because domestic logistics networks are operating normally without reports of staple hoarding or widespread retail stockouts. Natural gas and fertilizer risks track historical base rates closely, with low near-term probabilities that only rise gradually over longer seasonal windows.

What to Watch

  • Strait of Hormuz shipping volumes: Continued low transit counts maintain upstream pressure on crude and distillate availability.
  • Diesel crack spreads and freight rates: Sustained diesel prices above $6.00/gal are straining motor carriers, which could eventually feed into broader dry-van and grocery distribution costs.
  • Winter weather patterns: Approaching winter conditions could test heating demand in regions dependent on heating oil and natural gas.

What would change this view

  • Strait of Hormuz daily transit volume recovering toward the 60 ships per day baseline, which would sharply reduce medium-term crude and fuel risk.
  • A sudden 25% surge in retail gasoline prices over four weeks or widespread multi-state retail station outages lasting more than three days.
  • Widespread purchase limits on staple foods instituted by two or more national supermarket chains, or a monthly CPI food-at-home jump exceeding 2%.

What to do

  • Prepare Fuel (gasoline, diesel)
    Keep vehicle fuel tanks at or above half-full to hedge against local retail price volatility and delivery lags while diesel and freight markets remain tight.
