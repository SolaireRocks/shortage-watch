Overview

The Shortage Probability Index (SPI) sits at 64.4, remaining in the elevated band. The dominant driver of stress across US supply chains continues to be refined petroleum products. While physical fuel supplies continue to flow and consumer gas stations remain operational, prices for crude oil, gasoline, and especially diesel are heavily elevated above year-ago levels, exerting sustained upward pressure on logistics and freight networks.

Why These Probabilities?

  • Fuel (4% 1-week, 15% 1-month, 28% 3-month): Hard data underscores persistent cost pressures. Retail diesel is at $6.292 per gallon (+67.7% YoY) and regular gasoline is at $4.153 per gallon (+37.6% YoY). Benchmark Brent crude stands at $107.03 per barrel and WTI is at $99.64. The Strategic Petroleum Reserve stands at 282.2 million barrels, down 31.1% from last year, limiting federal buffer capacity. Additionally, freight carriers report contracting capacity and rising spot rates due to fuel surcharges. However, because qualifying fuel events (such as multi-state terminal runouts or formal rationing) historically require catastrophic physical disruptions like major hurricanes or cyber shut-offs, near-term outage probabilities remain low compared to the price pressure.
  • Food (0.3% 1-week, 1.5% 1-month, 4% 3-month): Upstream agricultural commodities show inflation, with international wheat at $317/mt (+37.3% YoY) and rice at $489/mt (+37.4% YoY). The FAO Food Price Index rose 5.8% YoY. Nevertheless, US domestic retail grocery supply chains remain well-stocked, and firsthand consumer hoarding indicators are flat. Qualifying national grocery rationing remains rare (roughly a 1-in-20-year baseline rate).
  • Natural Gas & Fertilizer (0.3% 1-week, 1.5% 1-month, 4% 3-month): Domestic gas data shows normal conditions (hard data index of -0.04). Fertilizer indices are flat at 0.0 with no current domestic supply breakdown, though global energy inputs warrant monitoring as winter approaches.

What to Watch

  1. Southern California Port Labor Talks: A potential strike by port watchmen and employers at the busiest US maritime hub could snarl inbound shipments and tighten container capacity.
  2. Strait of Hormuz Tanker Traffic: Prediction markets currently assign only a 4% chance that maritime transits rebound to normal levels (>60 per day) by late November, leaving global crude flows tight.
  3. Diesel Wholesale Spreads: Watch whether recent drops in diesel futures filter through to wholesale distribution racks to relieve struggling regional freight carriers.

What would change this view

  • A sustained reopening of the Strait of Hormuz to normal tanker volumes (above 60 transits per day), which would substantially relieve global crude prices and lower domestic fuel risk.
  • A prolonged multi-day labor strike shutting down major Southern California container terminals, which would propagate severe logistics delays and downstream freight supply crunches.
  • A sudden freeze or pipeline disruption forcing regional natural gas utilities to curtail residential service.

What to do

  • Prepare Fuel (gasoline, diesel)
    Keep primary vehicle fuel tanks at least half full to buffer against short-term price spikes and localized distribution friction caused by high diesel costs.
